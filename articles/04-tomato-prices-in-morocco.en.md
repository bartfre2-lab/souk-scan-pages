# Why did tomato prices rise in Morocco in autumn 2026?

*This article describes the period from late October to 20 November 2026. It reflects what was known on 23 November 2026. New facts may change the picture.*

Tomatoes are on almost every Moroccan table, so when the price jumps, everyone notices. This article sets out what the sources say, in three parts: what is well supported, what is debated, and what is not known. It does not blame any person or group. A price is the result of many choices by many people.

## What happened

In the last days of October, tomato prices rose. According to the weekly note of Casa Prestations, as reported by the press, the wholesale price in Casablanca on 29 October was 3 to 9 dirhams (DH) per kilo. The note before that gave 2.5 to 7 DH.

In early November, press reports gave shop prices of 15 to 20 DH per kilo in some cities. In Rabat, shop prices fell from 17 DH to 13-15 DH on 7 November, but some shops were still at 15 DH on 13 November. By 19 November, the wholesale price in Casablanca was coming down, with the top of the range at about 7.5 DH per kilo (Le Brief, 20 November).

Be careful when comparing these numbers. A wholesale price is what a shop pays. A shop price is what you pay. Both change by city, by quality and from day to day.

## What is well supported

**Less tomato than usual reached the market.** Every report we read agrees on this, though they give different reasons.

**Weather is named by several sources.** The president of FIFEL, the federation of fruit and vegetable producers and exporters, said the Souss-Massa region had four or five heat waves over the summer, which disturbed the flowering of the plants and reduced the possible harvest (EastFruit, 12 November). A government source quoted by the press said the early harvest was late because temperatures had fallen since October and tomatoes ripened more slowly (7 November). These are two different weather points: one about summer, one about October.

**The tomato virus is real and has been in Morocco for several years.** It is called tomato brown rugose fruit virus, or ToBRFV. It harms plants and leaves brown or wrinkled marks on fruit, which makes it unsellable (EPPO). It spreads through seeds, tools, clothing and hands. Morocco's plant health authority told EPPO that it first found the virus in October 2021 in Souss-Massa, and in March 2022 in Dakhla. About ten outbreaks in greenhouse tomatoes were confirmed in 2022-2023, and the authority linked them to infected imported seed. It is treated as an officially controlled pest.

On 11 November 2026, the Minister of Agriculture told a parliamentary committee that the tomato area in the Chtouka Aït Baha region had fallen by about half, from 8,000 to 4,000 hectares, according to the press (Le Brief, 20 November). Le Brief reported that the minister's warning centred on the virus.

## What is debated

**How much the virus explains this price rise.** We found no source that says the virus alone caused it. The FIFEL president said the virus adds pressure on top of the heat. Le Brief wrote that prices cannot be explained by the virus alone, since harvest delays, weather, transport costs, demand and exports also play a part. Published estimates of past losses from the virus vary widely and come from industry statements, not from official counts.

**The role of exports.** The president of the Casablanca wholesale traders' association listed strong export demand among the factors and suggested a temporary pause or a schedule for exports (reported by Hespress and relayed by AgriMaroc on 5 November). A trade publication wrote the opposite: that exports had fallen because supply was limited (EastFruit, 12 November). In early November, the authorities paused new export permits for tomatoes, onions and potatoes to African markets. The newspaper Libération reported that only new permits were affected, that earlier permits and shipments to Europe continued, and that Reuters had reported the measure on 5 November. The measure was presented at the opening of the government council on 12 November (LeSiteinfo, 13 November). Reports differ on exact dates and on how far the pause went. And the timing alone does not show what effect it had on prices.

**Production costs.** Producers and traders mention higher costs for fuel and inputs (Libération, 9 November). The FIFEL president put the cost of producing a kilo of tomatoes at about 5 DH. We found no independent measurement, so this remains a claim by the sector.

## What the authorities did

On 12 November, the head of government announced an inter-ministerial commission to follow price trends, daily price monitoring, and the temporary pause on new export permits described above. A report relayed from the press said the Ministry of Agriculture expected a gradual return to balance as seasonal production reached its peak (MADAR, 7 November).

## What is not known

**How much each factor counts.** We did not find any published breakdown that weighs heat, the virus, costs, the season and exports against each other.

**How big the shortfall was.** We found no official figures comparing this autumn's tomato volumes with a normal year.

**What happens between farm and shop.** Sources quoted different amounts for each stage, on different days and in different places. They cannot be added up reliably, so we do not use them. A transparent, checked breakdown would be useful.

**When prices will settle.** Producers quoted in the press expected larger harvests from late November, with a stronger recovery in December and January (EastFruit, 12 November). No official forecast was given. Wholesale prices had already started to come down by 19 November.

**What FAO says.** We looked for a statement from the Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO) on the Moroccan tomato market this autumn and did not find one. Nor did we find a ministry statement that explains the price rise in detail.

## A note on reading prices

A price seen in one shop on one day is a single data point. The same tomato can cost different amounts in the same city, depending on quality, location and time of day. That is why ranges and dates matter more than any single number.
