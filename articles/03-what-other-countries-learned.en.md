# What other countries learned about sharing prices

Picture a fishing beach in southern India in the 1990s. The boats come in at dawn. Fresh fish cannot wait, so the catch must be sold that day. But the price depends only on who is standing on that one beach. If many boats arrive together, the fish sells for almost nothing, and some of it is thrown away. On a beach a few kilometres away, there is too little fish and the price is high. Nobody on the first beach knows.

That is a problem of information, not of fishing. Researchers in several countries have asked what changes when people can see prices more easily. Here is what the evidence shows, and what it does not.

## Kerala, India: fishermen and mobile phones

The best-known study was written by the economist Robert Jensen and published in 2007 in the Quarterly Journal of Economics. He followed sardine fishing in three northern districts of Kerala from 1996 to 2001. Mobile phone service arrived in these districts at different times: 1997, 1998 and 2000. This allowed him to compare places with phones and places still without.

Once they had phones, many fishermen called several buyers on different beaches before deciding where to land their fish. The paper reports these changes:

- Before phones, on an average day about 5 to 8 percent of fishing crews could not sell their catch at all. Once phones were common, this waste disappeared.
- Prices on different beaches moved much closer together.
- On any given day, 30 to 40 percent of fishermen sold on a beach other than their home beach. Before phones, almost none did.
- Fishermen's profits rose by about 8 percent on average. The price paid by consumers for sardines fell by about 4 percent.

How can both sides gain? Because less fish was wasted. A fish sold somewhere is better for everyone than a fish thrown away. The author also estimated that a phone paid for itself in less than two months for the fishermen who bought one. Fishermen without phones gained too, because boats with phones moved to other beaches when the local catch was large.

The study has limits. It covers one fish, in one region, over five years. It compares areas over time; it is not a laboratory trial. The author himself notes that fish spoil quickly, which is why information mattered so much. He adds that roads and transport also matter, because knowing a better price helps only if you can reach it.

## Sri Lanka: the cost of not knowing

Sri Lanka has its own research, but it is about vegetables, not fish. Researchers at LIRNEasia, a policy research centre in Colombo, studied small farmers near Dambulla, the country's main wholesale vegetable market. The farmers grew tomatoes, onions, aubergines and chillies. The researchers found that about 11 percent of the farmers' total production cost went to finding information, such as prices, buyers and advice. Most of this was done by travelling and asking in person, and the farmers hardly used phones for it.

Please note what this shows. It measures how much it costs to be without information. It does not prove that farmers earned more once they had it. The researchers expected phones could reduce these costs, but that was their reasoning, not a measured result.

## India: price portals for farmers

India has also tried to make farm prices visible. Two platforms are worth knowing.

**Agmarknet** is a government website, launched in 2000, that publishes daily prices and quantities arriving at wholesale markets. It is the closest to the idea of a public reference price. We did not find a study that measures what it did for farmers' incomes, so we cannot say how large its effect was.

**e-NAM** (the National Agriculture Market), launched in 2016, goes further. It links existing wholesale markets into one online trading platform, so a buyer in one place can bid on produce in another. A 2025 study in the Mysore Journal of Agricultural Sciences looked at eight e-NAM markets in Haryana from 2015-16 to 2020-21. The number of deliveries to these markets grew. Price changes were mixed: some crops sold for clearly more, others for less. The study also found little competition between bidders, and most deals were still made in the old physical auction, with the result entered on the platform afterwards. It names reasons such as traders' doubts about online bidding, an uneven number of traders from one market to another, and digital systems for weighing and payment that were not yet fully connected.

So the Indian evidence is more cautious than the Kerala story. A platform can exist and still not change much if people do not use it in the way it was designed.

## What the lesson is

Putting the studies together, three things seem to matter.

**The information must be timely.** In Kerala, fishermen learned prices on the morning of the sale, when they could still act. A price that is a week old helps much less when the fish must be sold today.

**It must be trusted.** Jensen noted that deals made by phone were rarely broken, because fishermen and buyers both cared about their reputation. People use information only when they believe it.

**It must reach both sides.** In Kerala, buyers and sellers both had phones. In India's e-NAM, low participation by bidders limited the results. Price information that only one side can see tends to help that side only.

And it is not magic. Prices still depend on weather, transport, quality and the season. Information does not make a poor harvest bigger. What it can do is help people decide with a clearer picture, and reduce waste caused by not knowing.

## And SOUK-SCAN?

SOUK-SCAN is a small app that tries to put these lessons to use in Morocco. It combines reports from ordinary people into price ranges for everyday produce, and shows weekly wholesale reference prices published by Casa Prestations. A range is not a verdict on anyone. It is a starting point, so that buyers and sellers can talk about prices with the same information in hand.
